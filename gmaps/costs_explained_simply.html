Imagine you sell plates of momos. Guests hand you money. You spend some of it to keep the stall running. What is left over is yours.

If costs are bigger than sales, you lose money. If sales are bigger, you win. Everything below is about the costs.

Costs that stay, costs that move

Some costs never change. Others change with every guest.

Fixed costs

You pay these even if nobody comes. Think of school fees: due every month, even in the holidays.

  • Rent
  • Staff pay
  • Electricity, gas, water
  • Repairs, licences, software

Variable costs

These grow with every plate you sell. Think of pocket money: you only spend it when you go out.

  • Ingredients: flour, chicken, veg
  • Card and UPI fees
  • Takeaway boxes

What each plate costs you

Out of every ₹100 a guest pays, here is where the first part goes.

₹30
₹68 left

₹30 goes on ingredients. Flour, chicken, vegetables, sauces, oil. Spilled sauce, dropped momos and staff meals add a little more, which is why we plan for about 30 and not 25.

₹2 goes on payment fees and boxes. When a guest pays by card or UPI, the bank takes a tiny slice.

₹68 is left. It is called the contribution margin. Every sale puts ₹68 toward rent, the team and, in the end, your profit.

How many guests until you stop losing?

This point is called break-even: sales exactly pay all your costs, so you neither win nor lose.

Fixed costs are ₹1,11,000 a month, and each ₹100 sale puts ₹68 toward them. You need about ₹1.63 lakh of sales a month, which is about 10 guests a day.

The 10 hollow circles pay for the room, the team and the gas. The 15 filled ones are profit. After break-even, each extra guest puts about ₹375 in your pocket.

The piggy bank: ₹3,33,000. This is three months of fixed costs, saved for slow days. When it rains in Goa fewer tourists come, but the rent does not shrink. With it, the total is about ₹11.5 lakh.

Why Goa has three kinds of months

Your costs stay the same but your guests do not, so one month's profit cannot be multiplied by 12.

Peak
November to March, about 25 guests a day. About ₹1.7 lakh profit a month.
In-between
April, May, September, October, about 15 guests a day. About ₹57,000 profit a month.
Monsoon
June to August, about 8 guests a day. A loss of about ₹21,000 a month, paid from the piggy bank.

Add all twelve months and the best case is about ₹10 lakh a year. Plan on about two-thirds of that, since new restaurants nearly always start slower than hoped.

Costs people forget

GST
Guests pay 5% extra on the bill. That money belongs to the government, so it is not part of your sales. Decide early whether your menu prices already include it.
Your own pay
The numbers above do not pay you a salary. If you work there every day, set a number aside, or your profit is really just your wages.
Delivery apps
Zomato and Swiggy keep about ₹20 to ₹25 of every ₹100. You only keep ₹68 of each sale to begin with, so that slice is huge.
Wear and tear
Fridges break, woks crack, plates chip. Put money aside slowly to replace them.
Waste
Food that spoils or gets over-portioned is money in the bin. Count your stock every week.

Five things to remember

  1. Sales minus costs is profit. If it is negative, you are losing money.
  2. Fixed costs come first. You owe ₹1,11,000 a month before the first guest walks in.
  3. Know your break-even. About 10 guests a day covers every cost. Everything after that is profit.
  4. Keep ingredients near 30% of sales. Weigh portions and track waste, or the number creeps up.
  5. Save a piggy bank. The slow months will come, and the rent will not wait.

Wages, deposit, interiors, equipment and utilities are estimates, not quotes. Profit shown is before the owner's pay, income tax and equipment replacement.
